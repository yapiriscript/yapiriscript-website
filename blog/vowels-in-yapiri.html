Diacritics in Yapiri — How Vowels Are Marked

In Yapiri, vowels are not mere attachments — they are full citizens of the script, equal in standing to every consonant. Discover the elegant system that gives every sound its rightful place.

The title of this post contains a small irony. Ask "how are vowels marked in Yapiri?" and the most accurate answer is: they are not marked at all. They are written. Every vowel in Yapiri is a full, independent character — not a stroke added above a consonant, not a small symbol tucked below, not a diacritic of any kind. A vowel in Yapiri is just a letter.

That sounds obvious until you understand how differently most South Asian and Southeast Asian scripts treat vowels — and how radical a departure Yapiri's approach actually is.

The Abugida Default

Most scripts used for languages in and around Tripura are abugidas: writing systems where each consonant carries an inherent default vowel, and other vowels are indicated by diacritical marks attached to the consonant. Bengali script, Devanagari, and the Aima script for Kokborok all work this way.

In an abugida, the consonant is primary. The vowel is subordinate — a modification of the base form. You see this visually: the consonant is large and clear, the vowel diacritic is smaller, positioned above or below or alongside. The hierarchy is built into the shapes themselves.

Yapiri's Six Vowels

Yapiri has six vowel letters, covering the full phonemic vowel inventory of Kokborok. Each is a standalone character. None depends on a consonant to exist. You can write a word that is pure vowels — because in Yapiri, vowels are simply letters.

 a U+E000
 e U+E001
 i U+E002
 u U+E003
 o U+E004
 ə (schwa) U+E005

The sixth vowel — the schwa /ə/ — deserves special mention. It is the neutral, central vowel that appears frequently in Kokborok and is one of its most characteristic sounds. In Bengali or Roman transcription, it is often rendered imprecisely or omitted entirely. In Yapiri, it has its own dedicated letter at U+E005, given equal standing with every other vowel. The schwa is not an afterthought; it is a first-class character.

"Vowels are not decorations. They are the breath of the language — and in Yapiri, every breath is written down."

— Design principle, Yapiri v1.0

What This Means in Practice

The consequence of treating vowels as full letters is that Yapiri is a fully phonemic alphabet — every sound spoken is represented by exactly one character, and every character represents exactly one sound. There is no implied vowel to guess, no cancellation mark to suppress an inherent vowel, no context-dependent behaviour. What you see is what you say.

This makes Yapiri genuinely easier to learn than an abugida for native Kokborok speakers. The script follows the same logic as speech: you say /k/ then /i/, you write the /k/ glyph then the /i/ glyph. The correspondence is one-to-one. Reading aloud becomes a matter of sounding out each character in sequence — no hidden rules, no exceptions rooted in Sanskrit phonological logic that does not apply to Kokborok.

Key Point The full phonemic design of Yapiri is explored in depth in why Yapiri is a phonemic alphabet. This post focuses specifically on vowels and how the absence of diacritics is itself a deliberate design choice, not an oversight.

So Does Yapiri Have Any Diacritics at All?

Technically, no — in the traditional sense of marks that modify a base letter, Yapiri has none. Every character in the primary 48-character set is a standalone letter that stands on its own.

The script does include one additional character that serves a typographic joining function: the hyphen at U+E02E, used between reduplicated words. Kokborok uses reduplication extensively as a grammatical device, and the hyphen gives that grammatical boundary a clear, dedicated visual mark. It is not a diacritic in the phonological sense — it does not modify the sound of any letter — but it does serve a structural role that goes beyond ordinary punctuation.

Everything else in Yapiri is simply a letter: vowels and consonants, each standing equally, each mapped to one sound, each given full visual weight on the page.

The Deeper Meaning

The way a script treats vowels is a window into the assumptions it carries. An abugida trained on Sanskrit phonology treats consonants as the essential elements of syllables — a reasonable model for Sanskrit, where vowels are often predictable from context. But Kokborok is not Sanskrit. Its vowels are not predictable. Its schwa in particular is phonemically contrastive — change the vowel and you change the meaning.

A script that reduces Kokborok's vowels to diacritical marks is importing a model that does not fit. Yapiri was designed from the phonology outward — from Kokborok's actual sound system, not from any borrowed convention. The result is a script where nothing is hidden, nothing is secondary, and no part of the language is treated as less important than any other.

 kokborok — eight letters, eight sounds, none implied Every vowel written. Every sound visible. Nothing hidden.

The Heart of the Matter

In Yapiri, every vowel stands tall as a full letter — equal in dignity to every consonant. This is not just an aesthetic preference. It is a structural commitment: that Kokborok's sounds all deserve to be written, that nothing about the language should be treated as secondary or implicit, and that the script should follow the language rather than forcing the language to follow the script.

In every word we write in Yapiri, that commitment is visible. Every vowel, standing alongside every consonant, breathing life into text — proudly, clearly, and completely.

You can try writing in Yapiri right now on the web keyboard. Notice how naturally each vowel falls into place beside each consonant — two equal letters, two equal sounds, the language as it actually is.
