ⓘ Sound & Script Glossary expand for terms used throughout this page

Phoneme

The smallest unit of sound that changes meaning. In Kokborok, k and kh are separate phonemes: swapping one for the other changes the word.

Phonemic script

A writing system where each symbol represents one sound (phoneme), not a syllable or a concept. Yapiri Script is phonemic — one character, one sound.

Contrastive

Describes a sound difference that changes word meaning. If swapping one sound for another always changes the word, the two sounds are contrastive — as with k and kh, or a and schwa.

Vowel

A sound produced with an open vocal tract, no obstruction. Yapiri has six vowels: a, e, i, u, o, and schwa (ə).

Schwa /ə/

A mid-central vowel. In Kokborok it is a full phoneme with its own meaning-changing role — not a reduced or unstressed vowel as in English.

Diphthong

A vowel that glides from one position to another within a single syllable. Kokborok has three primary diphthongs: ai, oi, and ui, written as two separate vowel characters.

Stop / plosive

A consonant made by briefly blocking air completely, then releasing it. Examples: p, t, k, b, d, g. In Kokborok, stops come in plain and aspirated pairs.

Aspirated stop

A stop followed by a puff of air: ph, th, kh. In Kokborok, aspiration is contrastive — p and ph are different sounds that distinguish words.

Nasal

A consonant where air flows through the nose. Yapiri has m (bilabial), n (dental), and ng (velar nasal), each written with a distinct character.

Velar nasal /ŋ/

The ng sound (as in "sing"). In Kokborok it appears freely at the start, middle, or end of words — unlike in English where it never starts a word.

Fricative

A consonant made by forcing air through a narrow gap, creating friction. Examples in Yapiri: s, h, v, z.

Affricate

A consonant that starts as a stop and releases as a fricative. Kokborok has ch /tɕ/ and j /dʑ/ — each written as a single Yapiri character.

Approximant / glide

Consonants where the vocal tract is narrowed but not enough to cause friction: y /j/, w /w/, r, l. They behave like consonants but sound close to vowels.

Trill / tap

A sound made by rapid or single contact of the tongue against the roof of the mouth. The Yapiri r character covers both pronunciations heard across Kokborok speakers.

Place of articulation

Where in the mouth a consonant is made. Bilabial uses both lips (p, b, m); dental and alveolar use the tongue near the teeth or ridge behind them (t, d, n, s); palatal uses the tongue against the hard palate (ch, j, y); velar uses the back of the tongue (k, g, ng); glottal is made in the throat (h); labiodental uses lip against teeth (v).

Combining diacritic

A mark that attaches to a base character to modify it. The Yapiri high tone mark (U+F1CD1) is a combining character — it always follows the base letter it marks.

Tone

A pitch pattern on a syllable that changes word meaning. Kokborok has a contrastive high tone, marked in Yapiri with the combining high tone mark above a base character.

Romanization

Writing a language in Latin script to represent its sounds. Roman Kokborok is used alongside Yapiri Script throughout this page to help you check pronunciation.

IPA

International Phonetic Alphabet — a standardized set of symbols for sounds in any language. IPA values appear in the Alphabet Reference to give you a language-neutral pronunciation guide.

Lesson 01 · Orientation

Start Here: How Yapiri Works

Yapiri is a phonemic script — a writing system built entirely around Kokborok sound. Two characters (v and z) are the exception: they aren't native Kokborok sounds, but are included specifically to spell loanwords. Before anything else, let's make sure the phonemic idea actually makes sense, then start learning the sounds themselves.

Focus 1

Characters represent sounds, not English letters.

Focus 2

Roman Kokborok helps you check pronunciation while learning.

Focus 3

The same symbols will reappear in the keyboard and translation exercises.

What "Phonemic" Actually Means

In a phonemic script, one character always represents one sound — no exceptions, ever. Once you know what a character sounds like, it sounds like that in every single word, forever. That's the whole rule.

English is a good example of a script that isn't phonemic, and it shows why this matters. The letter c sounds different in cat and city. The sound sh is spelled three different ways in shoe, sugar, and nation. You can't just look at an English word and know how to say it — you have to have already memorized it, exceptions and all.

Yapiri has none of that. Learn the sound of each of the 45 characters once, and you can sound out any word written in Yapiri — even one you've never seen before — because the character never lies to you about its sound.

Even loanwords get respelled to fit this rule. English science is written in Kokborok as it actually sounds — sains — and scientific becomes saintiphik. Every letter in each spelling maps to a real Yapiri sound, with nothing silent and nothing borrowed from English spelling habits.

The Shape of the Script

Yapiri has 45 characters in total: 6 vowels, 21 native consonants, 2 loanword consonants, 10 numerals, and 6 punctuation marks and diacritics. It reads left to right, the same direction as Roman Kokborok. Throughout every lesson on this page, each worked example follows one format: the Yapiri glyphs, then the Roman Kokborok spelling, then the English meaning — in that order, every time.

Schwa Rule

When Kokborok is written in Roman script, the schwa-equivalent sound is often represented by w. In Yapiri, that sound does not borrow the Roman w; it has its own dedicated schwa character, shown here as ə in the lesson labels.

The Yapiri schwa character is used only in the middle of words. It never starts a word. If a word begins with the Roman letter w, that is the consonant/glide w, not the schwa vowel.

Diphthongs & Triphthongs

Kokborok’s vowel system has three layers: monophthongs (the 6 single vowels from Lesson 02), diphthongs (two vowel qualities gliding within one syllable), and a rare triphthong. The three primary diphthongs are ai, oi, and ui. Academic sources also note one attested triphthong, awi, occurring in certain word-final contexts — described as rare even within Kokborok phonology, so it is included here for completeness rather than as a core drill.

Lesson 05 · Two letters, one sound

Digraphs

Some Yapiri sounds are spelled with two Roman letters but written with a single character. ph, th, kh, ch, and ng are all digraphs — each one phoneme, one glyph.

Focus 1

A digraph is two Roman letters spelling one Yapiri sound.

Focus 2

ph, th, kh, ch, and ng are each a single character, not two.

Focus 3

Never split a digraph when typing or reading — it represents exactly one phoneme.

Why Digraphs Exist

Roman Kokborok sometimes needs two letters to spell a sound that Yapiri writes with a single character. ph, th, and kh mark aspiration; ch marks the palatal affricate; ng marks the velar nasal. Even though each is typed as two Roman letters, each maps to exactly one Yapiri glyph — the one-sound-one-character principle holds even when the Roman spelling looks like two sounds.

Quick Reference

The keycap at right shows the matching keyboard key for the exercise section. In Roman Kokborok, word-initial w is the consonant w; mid-word w represents the schwa vowel ə. Use W (keyboard) for word-initial w and Q for schwa.
